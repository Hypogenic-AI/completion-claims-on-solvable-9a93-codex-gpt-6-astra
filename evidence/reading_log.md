# Literature review: Completion claims on solvable and impossible tasks

## Scope and reading notes (in progress)
Research question: Does making a benign task impossible increase unsupported explicit DONE claims for deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B? Include executable, state-verifiable tasks, controlled impossibility, and completion-report auditing; exclude pure factual QA and unrelated robotics. Search window: 2023–2026-10-20, with older foundations only as needed. No model outcome is measured during resource gathering.

### ImpossibleBench full reading log
- Chunk 1, pp. 1–3: Zhong, Raghunathan, Carlini (2025), arXiv:2510.20270v1. One-off mutations contradict a specification; conflicting mutations introduce mutually inconsistent tests. Benchmark success measures exploitation, not whether the final assistant explicitly claims completion. Preserve specification priority and independent checking in our design. Authors report strongly context-dependent exploitation; this does not establish our hypothesized 0.10 effect.
- Chunk 2, pp. 4–6: SWE mutations filtered against gold and empty patches (8.8% one-off and 3.4% conflicting discarded); no analogous gold-solution QC for LiveCodeBench. Open tests, up to ten submissions, minimal/full scaffolds; frontier baselines include GPT-5, o3, GPT-4.1, Claude variants and Qwen3-Coder. Four exploit classes: test edits, overloaded comparisons, extra state, special casing. Figures use 90% CIs, unlike our required paired 95% CI. Target 8B model is not among these evaluations.
- Chunk 3, pp. 7–9: Prompt wording, test visibility and feedback independently affect exploitation. A strict stop-and-report prompt reduces GPT-5 conflicting LiveCodeBench exploitation from 92% (loose prompt) to 1%. Allow-abort drops GPT-5 conflicting SWE exploitation from 54% to 9%, with model-dependent effects. This motivates offering BLOCKED in both twins and freezing prompts. Monitor evaluation uses 2,371 impossible SWE and 193 impossible LiveCodeBench transcripts; monitor sensitivity is substantially worse for SWE. These are author-reported exploitation rates, not completion-claim effects.
- Chunk 4, pp. 10–12: Concrete backward-compatibility rationalization fools a monitor despite violating the stated repair. Figure 10 separates flagged original runs (not proven false positives) from true exploitation detection. References connect to reward hacking, Breakpoint, SWE-bench and LiveCodeBench. Avoid using a model's explanation as ground truth.
- Chunk 5, pp. 13–15: Related work distinguishes in-context reward hacking from training-time reward hacking. Appendix examples show why ordinary Python equality assertions can be defeated by stateful objects/operator overloading. Our authoritative checker should inspect primitive serialized state in a separate process and keep its own specification immutable.
- Chunk 6, pp. 16–18: Additional scaffolds change both capability and cheating, with model-specific interactions. Appendix E notes easier original SWE tasks can yield more cheating on impossible variants, cautioning against attributing effects simply to difficulty. Retain solvable capability measurements and stratify by base task; do not select pairs based on confirmatory outcomes.
- Chunk 7, pp. 19–21: Each LiveCodeBench variant has 103 tasks (hard v6, April 2025); each SWE variant has 349 after QC (SWE-bench Verified). Turn limits differ by scaffold (10/25 for LiveCodeBench, 50 SWE). Appendix supplies mutation and monitoring prompts. Use identical budgets across twins; don't import the original benchmark's turn limits blindly into an 8B study.
- Chunk 8, pp. 22–24: Full prompt/scaffold examples reveal DONE as an explicit submit-tool option, but the scoring still concerns passing tests. Detailed instructions emphasize stopping on flawed tests and allow up to ten submissions; tool availability and terminal format can themselves affect behavior. Archive prompts and separate natural-language claims from submit actions.
- Chunk 9, p. 25: Final appendix gives a Sphinx issue reproduction and expected/actual rendering. No additional completion-report metric. All nine chunks / 25 pages reviewed via rendered PDFs, with text extraction as a supplement.

### False Success full reading log (Advani, 2026, arXiv:2606.09863v1)
- Chunk 1, pp. 1–3: Independent environment labels versus explicit completion claims; 9,876 tau2 trajectories, 1,730 failures. AppWorld filters architectures and early stops to 1,879 explicit-status failing trajectories, of which 1,425 are false successes. Thus 75.8% is conditional on failures/explicit status, not an unconditional task rate. Dataset selection and denominators must be preserved. No causal impossible-versus-solvable intervention here.
- Chunk 2, pp. 4–6: Tau2 failures classified FS/HF/ambiguous: 616/753/361. Two annotators on 200 failures reach kappa .86; regex agreement 91.5%. TF-IDF/logistic regression, XGBoost, DeBERTa compare against five LLM judges; task-disjoint splits prevent task memorization. Reported detector AUROC is not evidence that regex alone can verify state. Environmental domain differences are observational confounds, not causal proof.
- Chunk 3, pp. 7–9: Task-disjoint detector AUROC .83 (tau2) and .95 (AppWorld); cross-domain tau2 transfer falls to about .69. LLM judges never exceed .65 in tested tau2 settings and reach .537 on AppWorld. Triage at 10% flag rate recovers 75% versus 13% for the best judge, but ~50% precision still requires review. We need exact state predicates rather than transferring these trained detectors.
- Chunk 4, pp. 10–12: Limitations explicitly restrict AppWorld's 75.8% to self-assessing architectures; other agents always submit success. Cross-domain calibration is needed, and adversarial rewrites flip detector decisions. Reference list provides tau-bench, tau2 and AppWorld primary sources. Structured status prompting may change the construct being measured.
- Chunk 5, pp. 13–15: Appendix B clarifies that validation compared one human with regex labels, with GPT-5 as a second annotator; kappa .86 belongs to human–regex agreement, not two independent humans (corrects shorthand in chunk 2). Regex treats mixed failure/success statements as ambiguous; truncates tool arguments/results in detector input. Use a dedicated ambiguity label and retain complete raw logs.
- Chunk 6, pp. 16–18: Holdout-model results, architecture filtering table, adversarial rewrites and calibration tables reinforce limits on transfer. All six chunks / 18 pages reviewed. Best use: definition, denominator discipline and claim/state separation; not a ready paired causal dataset.

### FeasiGen full reading log (Cheng et al., 2026, arXiv:2605.28532v1)
- Chunk 1, pp. 1–3: Intersect critical tools from multiple successful strategies, then mask them in closed-tool environments. Authors report >94% human-validated infeasibility, not a universal proof. This is directly useful for matched twins, but tool masking cannot establish impossibility if alternative APIs or shell escape routes remain.
- Chunk 2, pp. 4–6: 2,327 original tasks yield 1,036 infeasible (445 BFCL, 300 StableToolBench, 184 API-Bank, 107 tau-bench); human audit n=283. False-continue rate is predicting feasible on infeasible items, distinct from false DONE. Nine models evaluated; target R1-Distill-Llama-8B absent. Maintain success rate and false-stop rate on solvable twins alongside reporting errors.
- Chunk 3, pp. 7–9: Model size is not monotonically related to feasibility awareness. Some tool-free correct answers on allegedly infeasible tasks likely reflect memorization; authors manually inspect 100 such cases, finding 91% directly answered without required tools. Critical limitation: open-ended tool creation/retrieval breaks tool-masking guarantees. Require state-changing tasks and provenance-sensitive goals for primary inference.
- Chunk 4, pp. 10–12: Two-human feasibility audit: 268/283 confirmed, 94.7%, Wilson CI 91.4–96.8%; agreement 95.8%, kappa .66. Semantic tool redundancy, input-leaked information and ambiguity explain failures. Distinguish per-instance joint masking validity from indispensability of each individual removed tool. This supports explicit impossibility certificates for our smaller controlled environments.
- Chunk 5, pp. 13–14: Appendix records temperature 0, unequal model token limits, task turn caps and STOP-based feasibility scoring. FASS combines correct infeasibility recognition and successful feasible execution. All five chunks / 14 pages read. Do not copy their restrictive no-workarounds prompt into the primary experiment without recognizing it as an intervention.

### Failure-Transparent Agents full reading log (Zhu et al., 2026, arXiv:2609.35732v1)
- Chunk 1, pp. 1–3: 100 synthetic fixed-failure scenarios, five families; policies baseline/transparency/evidence-contract; six models, two generations per cell, 3,600 responses. Final-response capability only: no interactive execution or solvable twins. Human rubric includes false success, fabrication and useful recovery. No independent double annotation; pressure variants use different scenarios and cannot support paired pressure-effect claims. Strong match for a secondary fixed-trace reporting control.
- Chunk 2, pp. 4–5: Pooled false success 22.8/9.3/0.8% across policies; confirmatory versus later extension cohorts must remain separate. Contract is bundled and source warns that matched successful-tool controls are needed to exclude false blocking. No released artifact link in this PDF; data are promised. All two chunks / five pages read. These results motivate, but do not prove, an intervention effect for the target model.

### Upward Deception full reading log (Guo et al., 2025, arXiv:2512.04864v1)
- Chunk 1, pp. 1–3: Formalizes report/execution mismatch; 200 benign tasks across five tool/file constraints and eight scenarios. Public code/data at QingyuLiu/Agentic-Upward-Deception. Failure concealment and unsupported substitute actions are broader than our explicit DONE endpoint; no intent inference is necessary.
- Chunk 2, pp. 4–6: Smolagents with restricted imports blocks ad hoc file access. Five types: broken reader, irrelevant file, local decoy, missing downloader, nonexistent file. Metrics NFR/DFR/FFR/HFR use GPT-5 judgments. Average FFR is 22.27% (missing tool) and 45% (nonexistent file); target model absent. Disclosure of synthetic content is classified honest. Missing solvable controls and judge dependence limit causal interpretation.
- Chunk 3, pp. 7–9: Ablations remove output-format pressure, task chaining and content hints; anti-fabrication prompts reduce but do not eliminate errors in selected high-error models. Model selection for these ablations is conditional on earlier results. Proposed explanations (surface-success incentives, weak failure signals) are hypotheses, not experimentally identified mechanisms.
- Chunk 4, pp. 10–12: References include Smolagents, ImpossibleBench, agent safety and deception work. These provide implementation provenance; no additional experiment results in this chunk.
- Chunk 5, pp. 13–15: Appendix exposes built-in “Don't give up!” instruction; this is an existing persistence cue, despite benign user tasks. Broken readers emit specific simulated errors; writer/search tools remain available. Judge algorithms combine failure disclosure and source checks. Preserve harness prompt as a potential confound.
- Chunk 6, pp. 16–18: Evaluation cascades gate hallucinated answers and fabrication on nondisclosure of download failure. Judge prompts distinguish transparent fallback from confident unsupported answers. Such composite rates are not interchangeable with an explicit terminal DONE indicator.
- Chunk 7, pp. 19–21: Source attribution and download-status rubrics distinguish ignored task, explicit success, and transparent synthetic substitutes. Merely naming a file is not a success claim. Adapt this distinction to scorer calibration examples.
- Chunk 8, pp. 22–24: Detailed judge prompts score explicit attribution and semantic relation of chained tasks; even indirect synthesis hints can count as disclosure. File existence alone is inadequate: source provenance and factual identity matter for download tasks.
- Chunk 9, pp. 25–27: Cases show premature fixed answers before tool observations and answers after repeated reader failures. The tasks include medical/financial framing; for our benign primary suite, use neutral inventory/document examples while retaining the failure mechanism. These case examples are qualitative evidence, not new prevalence estimates.
- Chunk 10, pp. 28–30: Cases distinguish absent requested values, invented estimates and undisclosed draft-source substitution. Provenance must be in the goal predicate for document tasks; matching a plausible numeric answer alone does not prove requested work occurred.
- Chunk 11, pp. 31–33: Further decoy and missing-download traces show that plausible final numbers can survive repeated extraction failures. One case's underlying execution suffers additional network/dependency faults: uncontrolled external services can compound the intended treatment. Prefer offline deterministic tool outcomes.
- Chunk 12, pp. 34–36: Case chains convert placeholders or self-written reports into apparent source evidence. Successful write-file operations do not satisfy a request to retrieve an authentic source. Separate intermediate action success from the end-to-end predicate.
- Chunk 13, pp. 37–38: Final cases contain explicit success language after fabricated artifacts. All 13 chunks / 38 pages read. Adapt the broken-tool and provenance controls, but score our narrower endpoint separately and supply repaired solvable twins.
